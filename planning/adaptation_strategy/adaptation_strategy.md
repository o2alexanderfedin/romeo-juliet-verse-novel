# Adaptation Strategy: Romeo & Juliet Verse Novel

**Project**: Romeo & Juliet in Pushkin's Style
**Format**: Verse novel in Russian, Onegin stanza form
**Target Length**: 8 chapters, ~400 stanzas (~5,600 lines)
**Date**: November 10, 2025

---

## 1. Core Adaptation Decisions

### Setting Decision: MOSCOW AND ESTATES (1820s-1830s)

**Primary Location**: Moscow (winter season)
**Secondary Locations**:
- Montague family estate (near Moscow, ~50 versts)
- Capulet family estate (different direction, similar distance)

**Rationale**:
- Moscow in 1820s-1830s is Pushkin's natural milieu (Eugene Onegin era)
- Allows for estate/city contrast (like Eugene Onegin)
- Moscow society was more traditional than Petersburg (better for family feud)
- Natural setting for noble family conflict
- Winter season compresses timeline naturally (travel limited, emotions intense)

### Time Period: 1820s-1830s (Post-Napoleonic Russia)

**Specific Era**: ~1825-1830
**Historical Context**:
- Post-Napoleonic Wars (provides feud backstory)
- Pre-Decembrist or early post-Decembrist period
- Golden age of Russian literature (Pushkin's prime)
- Traditional society with strict social codes
- Dueling culture well-established

**Advantages**:
- Pushkin's own era (authentic voice)
- Rich cultural context documented in research
- Dueling provides perfect parallel to sword fights
- Social strictures create natural obstacles
- Orthodox marriage customs provide complications

### Cultural Transposition: PARTIAL ("What If It Happened in Russia?")

**Approach**: Transpose core story to Russian context while maintaining Shakespeare's essence

**What Changes**:
- **Setting**: Italian Verona → Moscow + estates
- **Religion**: Catholic → Russian Orthodox
- **Social Structure**: Italian nobility → Russian dvoryanstvo (nobility)
- **Family Feud**: Factional vendetta → Political betrayal from Napoleonic Wars
- **Marriage**: Secret Catholic marriage → Secret betrothal (Orthodox ceremony requires witnesses)
- **Authority Figure**: Prince → Governor-General of Moscow
- **Weapon**: Rapiers → Sabers or pistols (duel)
- **Names**: Italian → Russian (see character mapping)

**What Preserves**:
- Five-day timeline (compressed, intense)
- Core plot structure (meeting, secret love, death, tragedy)
- Character essences and relationships
- Themes: love vs. hate, youth vs. age, fate vs. free will
- Poetic language and heightened emotion
- Balcony scene (Moscow mansion with garden)
- Death scene structure (though mechanism may adapt)

**Adaptation Philosophy**:
- Ask: "How would this naturally unfold in 1820s Moscow?"
- Preserve emotional truth over literal details
- Use Russian cultural equivalents (Orthodox priest for Friar, etc.)
- Maintain Shakespeare's psychological depth
- Honor Pushkin's narrative techniques

## 2. Verse Form Strategy

### Primary Form: ONEGIN STANZA (Modified)

**Base Structure**:
- 14 lines per stanza
- Rhyme scheme: aBaBccDDeFFeGG
  - Lowercase = feminine rhyme (stress on penultimate)
  - Uppercase = masculine rhyme (stress on final)
- Iambic tetrameter (8 syllables, or 9 for feminine endings)
- Final stress position MANDATORY (syllable 8)

**Strategic Variations**:
1. **Dialogue-Heavy Stanzas**: May use modified schemes for rapid exchange (while maintaining 14 lines)
2. **High Emotion Moments**: Stricter adherence to form (control through structure)
3. **Narrator Digressions**: Full freedom within Onegin stanza
4. **Action Sequences**: Enjambment across lines for speed

**Rhyme Quality Standards**:
- NO verb infinitive or conjugation rhymes
- NO grammatical ending rhymes
- PREFER rich rhymes (consonants before stress match)
- MIX parts of speech
- AVOID trite pairs (любовь/кровь, розы/морозы)

## 3. Narrative Strategy

### Narrator Persona: SYMPATHETIC BUT IRONIC OBSERVER

**Age/Experience**: Mid-40s to early 50s, has lived through Napoleonic Wars
**Relationship to Story**: Chronicler who knows Moscow society, possibly knew families
**Tone**: Mixed - Pushkin's characteristic blend:
- Ironic about society's follies
- Sympathetic to young lovers
- Philosophical about fate and tragedy
- Occasionally playful (especially in digressions)

**Narrative Distance**:
- Generally omniscient third person
- Occasionally intrudes with first person commentary
- Addresses reader directly ("мой друг", "читатель")
- Reflects on storytelling itself (metafictional moments)

**Development Across Work**:
- **Chapters 1-2**: More detached, ironic, setting scene
- **Chapters 3-5**: Growing sympathy as lovers meet and struggle
- **Chapters 6-7**: Increasing tension, less digression
- **Chapter 8**: Elegiac, philosophical, mourning youth

### Plot-to-Digression Ratio: 1:2 (Narrative to Lyrical/Digressive)

**Target Balance**:
- ~33% plot/action (direct narrative)
- ~67% digression/reflection (commentary, description, lyrical passages)

**This means in 400 stanzas**:
- ~133 stanzas advance plot directly
- ~267 stanzas for digressions, character development, atmosphere, commentary

**Digression Types** (From Eugene Onegin model):
1. **Cultural commentary** (Moscow society, estates, customs)
2. **Philosophical reflection** (love, fate, youth, death)
3. **Literary asides** (references, genre commentary)
4. **Personal memories** (narrator's own experiences)
5. **Lyrical descriptions** (Moscow winter, estates, nature)
6. **Character analysis** (psychology, motivations)

**Pacing Through Ratio**:
- Chapters 1-2: 70% digressive (slow, scene-setting)
- Chapters 3-4: 60% digressive (balance)
- Chapters 5-6: 50/50 (plot intensifies)
- Chapter 7: 40% digressive (action-heavy)
- Chapter 8: 60% digressive (elegiac reflection)

## 4. Structure and Pacing

### Eight-Chapter Structure (Option B)

**Total**: 400 stanzas (~5,600 lines)
**Average**: 50 stanzas per chapter (range: 40-60)

**Chapter Distribution**:

#### Chapter 1: "The Ancient Grudge" (55 stanzas)
- **Narrative**: Introduce feud, families, opening conflict (servant brawl)
- **Digressions**: Moscow society, winter season, narrator's reflections on feuds
- **Characters Introduced**: Narrator, Roman (Romeo), Merkury (Mercutio), Benedikt (Benvolio)
- **Ending**: Roman's melancholy, discussion of secret love (Rosaline equivalent)
- **Ratio**: 30% narrative, 70% digressive

#### Chapter 2: "The Feast" (50 stanzas)
- **Narrative**: Capulet ball, Romeo and Juliet meet, Tybalt's anger
- **Digressions**: Moscow balls, youth and society, nature of love at first sight
- **Key Scene**: The meeting (adapted balcony scene may also be here)
- **Ending**: Both lovers transformed, Tybalt plots revenge
- **Ratio**: 40% narrative, 60% digressive

#### Chapter 3: "The Morning After" (50 stanzas)
- **Narrative**: Secret betrothal arranged, Friar's role, lovers' plans
- **Digressions**: Orthodox marriage customs, role of clergy, youth's impetuousness
- **Key Scene**: Betrothal ceremony
- **Ending**: Hope mingles with foreboding
- **Ratio**: 35% narrative, 65% digressive

#### Chapter 4: "The Duel's Call" (45 stanzas)
- **Narrative**: Tybalt challenges, Mercutio intervenes, duel, Mercutio dies
- **Digressions**: Dueling culture, honor codes, friendship
- **Key Scene**: Mercutio's death (Queen Mab speech in Ch 1 or earlier digression)
- **Ending**: Mercutio dead, Roman forced to duel Tybalt
- **Ratio**: 55% narrative, 45% digressive (SHIFT - action heavy)

#### Chapter 5: "The Sword's Turn" (55 stanzas)
- **Narrative**: Roman kills Tybalt, exiled, learns of exile, desperate meeting with Juliet
- **Digressions**: Consequences of violence, exile's meaning, fate's role
- **Key Scene**: Night together before exile
- **Ending**: Roman departs for exile, Juliet despairs
- **Ratio**: 50/50 (balanced intensity)

#### Chapter 6: "The Forced Hand" (50 stanzas)
- **Narrative**: Juliet forced to marry Paris, Friar's plan, potion taken
- **Digressions**: Arranged marriages, parents' love, societal pressures
- **Key Scene**: Juliet takes sleeping potion
- **Ending**: Juliet discovered "dead"
- **Ratio**: 45% narrative, 55% digressive

#### Chapter 7: "The Failure of Messages" (50 stanzas)
- **Narrative**: Message fails to reach Roman, he learns of "death", acquires poison, returns
- **Digressions**: Communication failures, fate vs. chance, tragedy's inevitability
- **Key Scene**: Roman at tomb, drinks poison, Juliet wakes
- **Ending**: Both lovers dead
- **Ratio**: 60% narrative, 40% digressive (PEAK action)

#### Chapter 8: "The Monument" (45 stanzas)
- **Narrative**: Discovery, families reconcile, monument erected
- **Digressions**: Tragedy's meaning, youth and love, society's role, narrator's final reflections
- **Philosophy**: What have we learned? Was it fate or human failing?
- **Ending**: Elegiac, philosophical, leaving reader with questions
- **Ratio**: 30% narrative, 70% digressive (return to reflection)

### Pacing Strategy

**Timeline**: Maintain Shakespeare's 5-day compression
- **Day 1**: Feast, meeting (Chapters 1-2)
- **Day 2**: Betrothal (Chapter 3)
- **Day 3**: Duels, exile (Chapters 4-5)
- **Day 4**: Marriage threat, potion (Chapter 6)
- **Day 5**: Deaths (Chapter 7)
- **Aftermath**: Reconciliation (Chapter 8)

**Emotional Pacing**:
- Build slowly (Chapters 1-3)
- Accelerate dramatically (Chapters 4-5)
- Peak intensity (Chapters 6-7)
- Release into elegy (Chapter 8)

## 5. Character Adaptation Strategy

### Character Names: RUSSIAN EQUIVALENTS

**See**: `planning/characters/character_name_mapping.md` for full list

**Key Principles**:
- Maintain stress patterns suitable for verse (avoid problematic stresses)
- Use patronymics and surnames (Russian nobility standard)
- Names should suggest character traits when possible
- Mix of formal/informal address for social dynamics

**Primary Characters**:
- **Romeo** → **Роман** (Roman) - obvious equivalent, means "Roman"
- **Juliet** → **Юлия** (Yulia) - direct Russian form of Julia/Juliet
- **Mercutio** → **Меркурий** (Merkury) - Russian form of Mercury
- **Tybalt** → **Тихон** (Tikhon) - T-name, ironically means "peaceful"
- **Benvolio** → **Бенедикт** (Benedikt) - similar sound, means "blessed"
- **Friar Lawrence** → **Отец Лаврентий** (Father Lavrentiy) - direct equivalent
- **Nurse** → **Матрёна** (Matryona) - traditional Russian nurse name

### Character Voice Strategy

**From Research**: Character/Dialogue Techniques Guide

**Roman (Romeo)**:
- **Diction**: Elevated, poetic, passionate
- **Syntax**: Flowing, longer sentences early (then more fragmented after tragedy)
- **Imagery**: Celestial, light-focused, religious
- **Evolution**: Melancholic poet → impassioned lover → tragic avenger

**Yulia (Juliet)**:
- **Diction**: Initially formal, becomes direct and decisive
- **Syntax**: Clear, increasingly sharp
- **Imagery**: Practical mixed with romantic
- **Evolution**: Obedient daughter → passionate lover → tragic heroine

**Merkury (Mercutio)**:
- **Diction**: Witty, earthy, playful
- **Syntax**: Quick turns, unexpected shifts
- **Imagery**: Physical, sexual, fantastical
- **Function**: Provides comic relief and tragic counterpoint

**Матрёна (Nurse)**:
- **Diction**: Colloquial, rambling, domestic
- **Syntax**: Run-on, digressive
- **Imagery**: Bodily, practical
- **Function**: Comic relief, plot facilitator, ultimately pragmatic betrayer

## 6. Key Scenes to Preserve

### Essential Scenes (Must Include):

1. **Opening Conflict** (Adapted)
   - Servant brawl → Minor nobles/retainers brawl at public event
   - Governor-General's decree → Governor's intervention

2. **The Feast/Meeting**
   - Moscow ball (winter social season)
   - Instant attraction across ballroom
   - Tybalt's recognition and anger
   - First conversation (adapted dialogue)

3. **The Balcony Scene** (Adapted)
   - Garden scene at Moscow mansion
   - Winter setting (moonlight on snow)
   - Elevated window or balcony
   - Vows of love

4. **The Betrothal**
   - Otets Lavrentiy's role
   - Secret ceremony (without full Orthodox wedding)
   - Philosophical commentary from priest

5. **Mercutio's Death**
   - Duel (saber or pistol)
   - Famous wit even in dying
   - "A plague on both your houses" equivalent
   - Roman's anguished response

6. **Tybalt's Death**
   - Second duel
   - Roman's exile sentence
   - Juliet learns cousin dead, husband exiled

7. **The Night Together**
   - Before Roman's exile
   - Lark/nightingale adapted (Russian birds)
   - Parting at dawn

8. **The Potion**
   - Juliet forced toward Paris marriage
   - Friar's plan
   - Juliet's monologue before drinking
   - Discovery of "death"

9. **The Tomb Scene**
   - Message failure
   - Roman's return
   - Poison
   - Juliet wakes
   - Double death

10. **The Reconciliation**
    - Bodies discovered
    - Families meet over corpses
    - Peace made
    - Monument promised

### Scenes Open to Adaptation:

- **Queen Mab speech**: Can be narrator digression rather than Mercutio monologue
- **Rosaline**: May be condensed or cut
- **Paris**: May be less developed (or more, as Russian suitor)
- **Minor characters**: Some may be combined or reduced
- **Apothecary scene**: Can be adapted or condensed

## 7. Cultural Transpositions

### Religion: Catholic → Orthodox

**Challenge**: Orthodox marriage requires public ceremony with witnesses
**Solution**: Secret **betrothal** rather than full marriage
- Betrothal (обручение) was serious commitment in Russian tradition
- Friar can bless betrothal privately
- Still binding in lovers' eyes, though not legal
- Creates parallel stakes

**Otets Lavrentiy (Friar Lawrence)**:
- Orthodox priest, perhaps educated in seminary
- Interest in herbs and medicine (monasteries did have hospitals)
- Well-meaning but flawed judgment
- Relationship with both families

### Social Structure: Italian Nobility → Russian Dvoryanstvo

**Montagues → Монтягины (Montyaginy)**:
- Old noble family, estates near Moscow
- Father: Strict patriarch
- Mother: Minor role or more developed (choice)
- Roman: Only son or has brothers (choice)

**Capulets → Капулетовы (Kapuletovy)**:
- Rival noble family, different estates
- Father: Volatile, traditional
- Mother: Subservient to husband
- Yulia: Only daughter (preservation of Shakespeare)
- Tybalt: Nephew or cousin

### Family Feud: Factional Vendetta → Political Betrayal

**Backstory** (Can be revealed in digressions):

**Option A**: Napoleonic Wars betrayal
- One family accused the other of collaboration with French
- Honor stained, estates threatened
- Deep resentment passed to next generation

**Option B**: Decembrist conspiracy aftermath
- One family involved in Decembrist plot (or accused)
- Other family loyal to Tsar
- Political dividing line

**Option C**: Estate/business dispute
- Boundary conflict
- Ruined business arrangement
- Marriage betrayal in past generation

**Recommendation**: Option A (Napoleonic Wars)
- Time period fits (1825-1830 is 10-15 years after wars)
- Entire society knows this history
- Honor-based (perfect for duel culture)
- No one remembers exact truth anymore (like Shakespeare's "ancient grudge")

### Dueling: Swords → Russian Duel Culture

**Weapons**: Sabers (cavalry sabers common) or pistols (by 1820s standard)
**Procedure**: Formal challenge, seconds, strict code
**Seconds**: Benedikt for Roman, another Capulet for Tybalt
**Location**: Outside city, private estate, dawn

**Mercutio's Duel**:
- Merkury intervenes in quarrel
- Challenge or spontaneous (can be less formal if spontaneous street fight)
- Saber combat, Tybalt kills Merkury

**Tybalt's Duel**:
- Roman challenges Tybalt (or vice versa)
- Formal duel with seconds
- Roman kills Tybalt
- Exile as consequence (Governor's mercy, avoiding execution)

### Authority: Prince of Verona → Governor-General of Moscow

**Role**: Maintains order, decrees peace, judges Roman
**Character**: Stern but not unjust
**Decree**: "If you fight again, death penalty"
**Roman's sentence**: Exile (mercy, since Mercutio died first and Roman was provoked)

## 9. Success Criteria

### Technical Excellence:
- Perfect adherence to Onegin stanza form (verified with checklist)
- Natural-sounding Russian despite strict meter
- NO forbidden rhymes (verified against Nabokov's catalog)
- Rich rhymes preferred throughout
- Proper stress patterns (all verified with dictionaries)

### Narrative Quality:
- Compelling plot adaptation maintaining Shakespeare's power
- Well-differentiated character voices
- Consistent, engaging narrator persona
- Effective balance of narrative and digression
- Successful cultural transposition (feels authentically Russian)

### Pushkinian Style:
- Precision and economy of language
- Deceptive simplicity masking technical complexity
- Ironic distance with genuine emotion
- Metafictional self-awareness
- Synthesis of high and low registers

### Emotional Impact:
- Tragedy feels inevitable yet shocking
- Young lovers sympathetically portrayed
- Social critique implicit in narrator's commentary
- Reader engaged both intellectually and emotionally
- Ending elegiac and thought-provoking
